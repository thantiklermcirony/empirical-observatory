# Ten-minute IDA observation pilot

This is a reproducible feasibility stimulus for measuring changes in EEG feature geometry, followed by quiet observation. It is not a validated fear test, personality assessment, lie detector, or calming treatment. Its purpose is to establish whether usable signals and interpretable responses can be obtained before choosing a stronger or longer protocol.

The deliverables are two 600-second MP4 files, exact event schedules, word norms, a source generator, and integration with the IDA Lab catalogue. Both videos use the same content. Version B reverses the word sequence and audiovisual condition order. This is a partial order control; it does not counterbalance every transition. The videos use a flat virtual cinema screen, not a moving 360-degree world.

## What the evidence establishes

There is no evidence-backed universal number of seconds that produces or holds a specified emotion in every person. Brief word processing, sustained emotional experiences, return of a physiological signal, and recovery of reported well-being are different outcomes. A review of every psychology book would not establish an exact timing constant. The design below uses a focused selection of original studies and datasets, with its own pilot choices explicitly identified.

In Bayer and Schacht's cross-domain experiment, words, pictures and faces were displayed for three seconds following a one-second mask. There were 24 items per valence category in each domain, repeated twice. Word categories were controlled for length, frequency and imageability. Effects differed between modalities; the EEG system had 61 electrodes. That experiment supports careful stimulus matching and repeated observations, not direct transplantation of a scalp ERP marker to a four-channel Muse.[^1]

The DEAP authors recorded 32 participants viewing 40 one-minute music-video excerpts and collected participants' affect ratings. This demonstrates a sustained audiovisual measurement paradigm. It does not prove that 60 seconds is necessary or sufficient, or that an arbitrary new clip induces the intended feeling.[^2]

Other primary datasets used two-minute films or approximately three-minute VR clips. Their procedures and content differ. The newer VR dataset prioritized narrative context rather than identical clip lengths. These findings argue against treating all stimulus classes as interchangeable or assuming a universal washout interval.[^3][^4]

Warriner and colleagues provide population ratings of valence, arousal and dominance for 13,915 English lemmas. The supplied word list uses their aggregate valence and arousal values, but the categories remain hypotheses about an individual's response. Equal word length alone does not control familiarity, grammatical role, arousal or personal associations.[^5]

## Mechanisms worth separating

This is a practical research map, not a catalogue of guaranteed ways to manipulate a mind. Each mechanism requires its own comparison and independent outcome.

| Mechanism / candidate contrast | What to manipulate | What could otherwise explain the EEG change | Treatment in this pilot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emotional meaning | Positive, neutral and negative words | Word frequency, reading, familiarity, personal relevance | Twelve four-letter words; incomplete matching is declared |
| Pictorial versus symbolic processing | Words versus faces or scenes | Color, spatial complexity, gaze and recognition | Pictures/faces reserved for a separately normed extension [1] |
| Context and perceived source | Identical words presented in different social contexts | Expectation, belief about the sender, task meaning | No deceptive personality feedback; future transparent context comparison [6] |
| Musical harmony and roughness | Harmonic versus closer-frequency combinations | Culture, preference, sensory responses, auditory beating | Original low-level tones; no universal emotion labels [7] |
| Temporal predictability | Regular versus irregular trajectories | Motion energy, attention and surprise | Slow abstract trajectories; a physical manipulation, not a verified fear induction |
| Audiovisual combination | Sound alone, image alone, both | Cross-modal sensory effects | Current scenes are compound probes; factorial isolation is future work |
| Narrative and suspense | Coherent unfolding events | Familiarity, editing, anticipation and identification | Excluded from first short pilot; requires longer clips and norms [3,4] |
| Appraisal and interpretation | Observe versus reinterpret the same material | Instruction-following and cognitive effort | Training extension, kept separate from assessment [8] |
| Repetition and order | Same items across sessions and positions | Learning, fatigue and expectation | Two reversed versions; more orders and unseen items needed |
| Response demand | Passive viewing versus target detection | Motor responses and workload | Passive viewing here; existing target task must be tested separately |
| Display and bodily context | Flat display versus headset | Screen size, head movement, contact, eye movements | Fixed virtual screen, no simulated locomotion |

In a cross-cultural experiment, Tsimane' listeners did not show the Western preference for consonance over dissonance. Musical associations cannot be assumed universal.[^7] A context experiment found that perceived sender identity and predictability affected processing of feedback words, illustrating why labels and framing are part of the stimulus.[^6] A reappraisal experiment used explicit instructions, practice and randomized image trials; its findings do not validate passive video as adaptive therapy.[^8]

No subliminal messaging, jump scares, graphic violence, threats to the participant, or sexual media are included. Ordinary negative words include fear, pain, loss and hate. The participant can review this list and decline. Greater intensity is not assumed to yield better information. The initial question is whether there is a repeatable response at modest intensity.

Order A presents ordered drift, regular orbit, then irregular motion. Order B reverses these. There is no instruction to become calm, anxious or afraid, and the condition names are not displayed. All scenes contain twelve outlined circles on the same dark-gray background. Their movement and tone combinations differ. This is not a comparison of scientifically established calm/neutral/fear states.

The word sequence in A is desk, hope, fear, fork, love, loss, tile, safe, pain, gate, calm, hate; B reverses it. Four words belong to each author-assigned category. No word is repeated within one video. Fixed ten-second cycles permit anticipation; a later randomized-interval protocol should test that dependency.

## Why these timing choices

**Baseline, 60 seconds:** a practical allocation for settling, checking contact and observing several feature windows. Discard the first 15 seconds from baseline summaries. This is not a guarantee of stationarity or of a well-estimated high-dimensional covariance. The current application requires its reference to be frozen separately before starting the video. Treat the video's baseline as an independent stability check against that reference, not an automatic recalibration.

**Word display, two seconds:** readable without rapid flicker, followed by eight seconds without another word. The 10-second cycle accommodates a four-second pre-onset feature window and a four-second early response window. The response window contains both the word and its removal; it is a coarse trial response, not a pure word-on-screen spectrum or a millisecond ERP. This timing is a design choice, not the exact protocol of [1].

**Scene exposure, 45 seconds:** five seconds of smooth appearance and audio fade-in, 35 seconds of full-level exposure, then five seconds of fade-out. Use 10–35 seconds after onset for a conservative sustained-response summary. A 45-second exposure does not mean the participant's emotion is held for 45 seconds. It holds a specified physical condition. Three short conditions fit a ten-minute feasibility run; later narrative studies may need longer durations.

**Quiet observation, 30 seconds:** allows early-return estimation without spending most of the pilot on rest. With the default IDA residue half-life of 30 seconds, zero further excess would halve residue over this interval. This is a mathematical consequence of the chosen dial, not a biological recovery constant. A 30-second interval cannot establish full recovery. For a clean 30-second comparison whose four-second windows both lie wholly within quiet recovery, more than 30 seconds of rest is needed; therefore use early slopes here and mark longer measures unavailable or mixed-context.

**Ratings, 15 seconds:** five seconds per question. Pleasantness and arousal refer to the preceding stimulus section; discomfort refers to the present moment. Ratings follow quiet recovery so speech or keypresses do not contaminate its immediate portion. This creates recall and recency bias: a future study should compare immediate ratings with a separate uninterrupted recovery condition. No score entered in five seconds means missing, never neutral.

**Transitions:** discrete onsets for words, fixed background throughout; smooth fades only for the audiovisual scenes. A continuous montage is a good future naturalistic task, but it makes attribution to any one word, sound or image difficult. Do not add background music beneath the word section.

## Measurement and analysis contract

Freeze chart selection, covariance/scale, tolerance delta and half-life before the trial. Record all candidate charts if desired, but designate one primary analysis before examining outcomes. Let d_m(t) be distance from that chart's frozen reference, e_m(t)=max(0,d_m(t)-delta_m), and r_m its normalized exponentially weighted residue.

For word onset o, define B=[o−4,o) and P=[o,o+4). Recompute raw EEG features on those exact windows, or use only logged windows that fit the intended boundaries. The online half-second feature grid is not generally aligned to video onsets. A scalar feature timestamp labels a trailing four-second window, not a point observation. Define reactivity as d_m(P)−d_m(B). Treat the four trials per category descriptively; do not use hundreds of overlapping windows as independent repetitions.

For scene onset o, report the mean d_m over wholly contained windows in [o+10,o+35]. Compare with the independent video baseline as the primary contrast. For local context, the clean pre-scene intervals are 260–270, 335–345 and 425–435 seconds respectively. The latter two precede rating periods, so they are not immediate pre-onset baselines. Never use the last four seconds immediately before scenes 2 or 3 as quiet rest; those seconds contain a rating prompt.

Report burden as the integral of e_m across a declared valid exposure/recovery interval. Do not interpolate over long gaps or treat missing signal as zero. Report valid coverage and use a preregistered pilot inclusion threshold, such as at least 80% coverage, explicitly marked provisional.

Recovery time is the first time distance is within delta for a declared five-second dwell, using windows wholly inside the quiet period. If that does not occur before rest ends, the result is right-censored. The first clean four-second recovery window ends four seconds after scene offset, limiting onset precision. Residue remains influenced by prior trials; do not reset it at every scene boundary to manufacture recovery.

The output should retain reactivity, burden, early return, censored recovery time, subjective ratings and data quality as separate quantities. This video contains no response-speed task; it cannot measure switching cost or sustained challenge performance. Use the application's separate target task for those outcomes. A change in alpha or distance during tones may reflect sensory stimulation, attention or auditory beating rather than emotion. The three scene designs are compound audiovisual manipulations, not isolated psychological mechanisms.

## How to use the files

1. Before showing the video, check comfort, ordinary reading ability, permitted word content, display and sound level. Record initial pleasantness, arousal, discomfort and sleepiness outside the timed file. Learn the three 1–9 rating scales first.
2. In IDA Lab, start real Muse acquisition, obtain clean input and freeze the reference during separate eyes-open quiet observation. Synthetic mode is only for plumbing tests.
3. Select Order A or B from Video library, then Play. Select **flat** projection even inside VR. Use a fixed virtual screen; do not map the movie onto a 360-degree sphere. Keep display brightness, audio gain, screen distance and headset fit unchanged across sessions.
4. During rating prompts, press 1–9 on the presenting computer. Values are archived only while acquisition is running. If answering verbally in VR, an observer must record the numbers in the provided worksheet; there is no speech transcription or VR numeric keypad. Exclude speech and movement intervals from EEG analysis.
5. Escape clears video on desktop. The XR squeeze action clears playback and exits VR. A participant stop ends the stimulus; recording can be stopped separately. These controls still require browser/hardware verification.
6. Stop acquisition and export the session. Preserve the movie's JSON schedule and hash. If a video is played outside the integrated catalogue, it does not automatically supply synchronized EEG markers or collect ratings.

The video runs for ten minutes, but setup, reference collection, initial ratings and any extra recovery make a real visit longer. If discomfort persists, end the test and allow untimed quiet recovery; do not continue merely to preserve the schedule. A fixed MP4 cannot adapt its duration to a person's state.

The browser integration uses video-frame callback metadata to log media time and the first observed frame in each cue. The browser API provides compositor timing, not a hardware measurement of photons, and can be late.[^9] The export does not remove Bluetooth timestamp uncertainty. No ERP timing claim is made until the actual Muse/display path is measured. The split computer-to-headset link does not yet synchronize video playback; do not use it for event-aligned conclusions from this test.

## Deciding whether to change the duration

First obtain pilot estimates of data loss, reported response, discomfort, repeated-session variability and the fraction of censored recoveries. If the physical signal is poor, fix acquisition before increasing intensity. If stimuli are rated emotionally neutral, replace or norm the content rather than assuming a larger IDA number means emotion.

A follow-up timing study should randomize 30-, 45- and 60-second exposures across separate sessions or participants, and compare 30-, 60- and 90-second observation periods. Choose the shortest protocol that meets predeclared precision, tolerability and repeatability targets on held-out data. Do not tune and report performance on the same run. A sample-size calculation requires pilot variance and a smallest useful effect; neither is currently available.

For confirmatory use, add more stimuli per class, further order assignments and independent sessions. Two reversed videos leave the regular condition in the middle and do not remove all carryover. Learning an adaptive calm-training policy would require randomized permissible actions, independent reports of calm and alertness, and comparisons with fixed content or replayed action sequences. That is a separate experiment.
